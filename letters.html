From Dad

On paying attention

Letter · I  ·  Spring 2026

Emma — there is a thing your mother and I have been trying to say to you for about eighteen years now, in a hundred small ways, and I am going to try to say it once more, plainly, while I have you sitting still.

Pay attention. Not to the loud things; the loud things are easy. Pay attention to who is quiet at the dinner table, and why. To the way the light falls in the kitchen at four o'clock in October. To the moment in a conversation when someone is about to say something true and is deciding whether you can be trusted with it.

Most of what makes a life worth living, I have come to understand, is not produced. It is noticed. The world is generous with people who notice it. It rewards them with weather, with strangers, with afternoons that don't make it onto any calendar but which they remember for forty years.

You are, and have always been, a noticer. Don't let anyone train it out of you. Not professors, not employers, not boys, not the internet. Keep your eyes the way they are.

I love you. I am proud of you. Drive carefully and call your mother.

From Mum

The small mornings

Letter · II  ·  April 2026

My darling girl. I wrote you a letter on the day you were born and put it in a drawer, and I have not been able to find it for a decade, so I will write you another one.

What I wanted to tell you, and still do, is that the small mornings are the ones that matter. The toast burned the same way every Tuesday. The hairbrush we fought over for six years. The lunchbox notes I stopped writing when you asked me to and then started again, smaller, when I thought you weren't looking. Those mornings are the marrow of a childhood, and they are yours now, fully — to remember in whatever shape you want.

I have spent eighteen years trying to make you brave and kind and reasonably tidy. Two out of three is a fine outcome and we will work on the tidy part by post.

Wherever you go from here, you are taking a great deal of us with you. Not as weight — as ballast. The kind that keeps a small boat upright in a strong wind. Use it. Lean on it. Come home when you need to and stay away when you don't.

I am so proud of you I can hardly stand it.

Your mother, always, — Mama

From Grandma Eva

A short note, with instructions

Letter · III  ·  March 2026

Emička — I am eighty-one years old and I have learned exactly four things worth passing on. Here they are, in no particular order, so you cannot lose them.

One. Eat something warm in the morning, even when you do not want to. The body is a small animal and it needs to be coaxed.

Two. Marry no one in a hurry. The good ones do not mind waiting; the bad ones reveal themselves if you give them long enough.

Three. Learn one piece of music well enough to play it from memory. There will be a day, far from now, when you will be glad to have it in your hands.

Four. Call your grandmother. She is, as a rule, sitting by the phone.

That is everything I know. I love you to the moon and the chickens.

In defense of the older sister

Letter · IV  ·  April 2026

Em — a few things, since I will not say them out loud and you will not let me write them in the card.

You taught me to tie my shoes, to ride a bike, to lie convincingly to our parents about who broke the lamp, and to apologise afterwards in a way that worked. These are, in order, the four most useful skills I have, and I have you to thank for all of them.

I have spent fifteen years pretending you were annoying. You are, in fact, the best person I know. I am not going to say it again, so make a note of the date.

When you go away, the house will be too quiet, and Bagel will lie in your doorway for a week looking offended. I will tell you about it on the phone. Try not to make it weird.

Go and be brilliant. Come back for Christmas. Bring snacks.
